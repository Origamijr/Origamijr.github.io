A Canadian survey of children in developing countries found that when asked what they would do if they were the president of their country, most children chose to say they would improve education.

Living in the United States, education is something I think many of us take for granted. Prior to the 18th century, education was largely private (see medieval cathedral school). The shift to education as we know it actually started with the Prussian education system. While publicly funded schools existed, this new model of education brought about grade levels and standard curricula.

From my limited reading of the subject, it's hard to pin on the motivations to publicize and normalize education. Several naive motivations can include increasing productivity of workers to perform more complex tasks with less training, creating an educated public to support a democratic country, or enabling the human autonomy of the citizens of the country.

Rapid cultural and technological developments have led modern discourse to reexamine the purpose and methodologies of education. Many studies indicate a literacy crisis in the United States, along with a "reverse Flynn Effect" in generation Z and alpha.

While pedagogy is not my primary interest, having spent my entire conscience life in school up to this point in time, I have been forced to reckon with the consequences and politics of the US education system. I also sometimes fantasize about one day becoming an educator of some sort, but that is a discussion for another day. So amongst this perceived modern education crisis, I want to step back and outline my ~~naive~~ philosophy of education.

Before diving deeper, it's worth touching on the nature of propaganda and indoctrination of education. I would believe most people can agree that these can be problematic, any policy discussion on this subject is haunted by the notion that indoctrination and education are simply two sides of the same coin. This dips a bit into critical theory, but anything we choose to educate is influenced by culture. That is to say there is no apolitical education. Under this assumption, it would seem that an ideal politicized education would be underlied by some sort of politics of "fairness", which hopefully fosters an awareness of the varieties of thought. In the most controversial parts of this discourse come into the notion of "harm", with cognitohazards (so called in fiction, but analogously applies to reality) and social contagion. This aspect of pedagogy is something I am admittedly unprepared to discuss, but I can hopefully develop my thoughts on this topic at a later date.

Above, I have already outlined several possible sociologically and economically advantages to an educated populace. Towards these goals, there's this somewhat pervasively known "4Cs" that are the 21st century framework for education. These 4Cs (creativity, collaboration, critical thinking, and communication) largely supplanted the "3Rs" (Reading, wRiting, and aRithmetic) as society became more critical of education methodology. While I agree with many of these notions, I'd like to offer my own revised 4C's

**Curiosity** is perhaps the most important but also the most challenging idea to integrate into education. Fostering curiosity offers innate motivation for learning, and enables continuous learning even outside the classroom. Being abstract, I'm not sure there is a universal way to instill curiosity in students. A teaching philosophy that I think may work is to enable and empower students to pursue topics and tangents of their own choice.

**Community** is important. We live in a society. In the past century schools have incidentally served as one of the key institutions in developing the social lives of children. An effective education should lean into this and foster greater community between students, and also with the culture and local community around them.

**Creativity** is my stand in word for independence. Modern education seems to have mostly become critical of "teacher infallibility", and moves towards student autonomy. Creativity is key for future student success, as they will often find themselves in situations where they'll have to trailblaze their own paths with either a lack of guidance, or a lack of precedence. In the other semantic sense of creativity, education should also offer students means to learn and examine their own self expression, in both the artistic and broader senses.

**Common Core** as a phrase has unfortunately became tied to a set of US K-12 standards that has come under the fire of parents the past decade. What I mean by Common Core is the necessary set of skills that schools must teach towards a functioning citizen, and as a baseline for future learning endeavors. Largely, I think many educational standards and curricula likely attempt to cover a bit too much, while both leaving out other essential core teachings (finance, government, etc) and not leaving enough space for student-first electives and project-based material.

A pervasive dilemma in education is how these outcomes should be measured. After all, we need to know when our goals are not being achieved, and where improvements can be made. The traditional measure for educational outcomes is standardized testing, but literature also looks to other metrics such as college acceptance rates and occupational outcomes. While the ideal utilitarian metric would look at the longest time scale, the immediacy to assess and help the current students necessitates more temporally local metrics, hence standardized testing.

In my view, standardized testing is something essentially everyone agrees is deeply flawed, yet outside of the alternative of it's abolishment, alternatives or methodological changes never seem to achieve their ideals. Standardized tests fall into the trap of Goodhart's Law, where a metric fails to be a good metric when actors start to use it as an optimization goal. Teachers teach to the test. Students prioritize tests over learning. The problems are apparent.

It would seem that in an ideal world, these perverse incentives can be eliminated by the abolition of standardized testing. We have already seen some private schools make this move, with their students still achieving good outcomes. As mentioned before though, in a public system where outcomes prompt measurement to justify budget allocation, a form standardized testing seems to be culturally required. As public education institutions are necessary in the current climate to ensure equity, we come into the current impasse. 

In essence at an individual outcome, we want to have curious students equipped with social skills and a set of core knowledge to further their own self-realization and independence, which collectively leads to a more free and productive society. The current education system is, however, haunted by perverse incentives towards goals not aligned to these desired outcomes. If we want to move towards better education, I believe a good (and necessary) step is a cultural change, where we take education granted for less, and parents care more about the educational outcomes of their children. Optimistically, policy and discourse still has a chance to steer the perception of education in this direction before we are forced by necessity to address these issues in a broken future where technology and culture outpace educational policy.
