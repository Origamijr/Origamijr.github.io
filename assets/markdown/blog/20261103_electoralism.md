1. The Change Voter: A displeasure in current establishment government, willing to take a chance with any type of change.
2. The Vibes Voter: Concern for their own most salient needs in a troubling socioeconomic environment, with one candidate leaning harder into populist messaging.
3. The Media Voter: Consumed by a very selective media environment, with an uncritical acceptance of delivered narratives.

All of this demonstrates a failure of the government to deliver (both materially and optically), and the partial myopia and willful political ignorance of the general citizen. Combined with rising political apathy and low voter turnout, we end up in an environment where democracy is co-opted by optics over policy.

## An Ideal Government

Before discussing the problems in the very notion of democracy, we should first consider the goal of democracy. Democracy is one way to determine and allocate the power of the government. To determine the best way to allocate this power, we should try to determine what even is the ideal government.

The role of government is highly contested, with thoughts ranging between small/no government (libertarian, anarchist) to big government (authoritarian). I'll leave the discussion of anarchism to another time, as I find it a fascinating topic that is completely beyond my understanding (I see smart people advocating for it, so I can't throw it out).

Under the framework of game theory, a government (a centralized authority) solves two key societal issues. First, a government can solve the tragedy of the commons, which is an economic dilemma where, if everyone acted in their own localized self-interest, the optimal choice for an individual results in a worse outcome for everyone (a common example of this is rationing and climate change). Second, governments prevent coordination failure by ensuring a degree of common knowledge (traffic laws, for example).

While that outlined a benefit of government, we still need to outline what a government ought to do. In my view, the government should serve several core purposes (ordered by importance):

1. Represent and act in the well-being of it's citizens
2. Provide guidance in the allocation and distribution of resources
3. Establish centralized rules necessary to guide peace and ethics
4. Maintain a reserve of resources and labor for times of need

Admittedly this ranking seems fairly arbitrary and based on my own moral instincts (other way of saying I'm not literate enough to word my justification). 

I place "serving the citizenry" as the first role of government as a government that doesn't act in service of its citizens would in a sense be dialectically contradictory. So long as we believe in a degree of unalienable human rights (including an assignment of value to autonomy), a government that does not put its population as priority will inevitably violate these rights.

In this way, democracy (rule by the people) would appear to be the most favorable system to ensure citizens are being served by the government. An autocracy (rule by one) or an oligarchy (rule by the few) would encounter conflict with inevitable misalignment when large swaths lack representation. 


## A Widening Chasm

A necessary condition for a functional democracy is an educated, sufficiently-informed populace. Should decision making be guided by ignorance, government will cease making decision towards the actual well-being of it's citizens.

In practice, as the role and duty of government expands, it becomes impractical to expect the population to be knowledgeable and participate in every decision in government. This is usually resolved by a combination of a representative republic, where the population elects officials who they believe will act in their best interests, and technocrats (rule by the expert) who make decisions in specialized domains. 

Even if only few figureheads hold the ability to directly enact change, a fair system must consider the wants and needs of the population. A fine balance must be struck between regular checks on the power of these decision makers, and permitting a degree of autonomy in decision making to allow their changes to take root and manifest.

However, we have seen this gap between the knowledge needed to run a functioning government and the knowledge of the general populace widen to a chasm. In part, this can be a sign of prosperity, where the citizen is privileged to no longer need to care what decisions a government makes, indulging in the liberty of knowing only what they want to pursue.

The problem arises when this gap in knowledge loops back upon itself as an uninformed populace votes using imperfect knowledge. Candidates no longer need to appeal to actual solutions and policies, since the populace no longer has the capability to verify their efficacy. They only need to **appear** as if they have effect solutions and policies.

Should this optics game turn malicious, we begin to enter an age of deliberate misinformation. This type of misinformation does not even require one to lie. One simply needs to control the narrative framing.

In a media environment where attention is coveted, we see the adverse effects unrestricted capitalism have on democracy. Mainstream news outlets are beginning to be consolidated under the monopoly of one individual (Skydance merger). Social media sites control who's politics gets recommended, and who's gets removed (Meta influencing Brazilian and Florida elections). Individuals can "buy" more voting power though unrestricted ad spending (Elon musk and other super PACs).

If I was a step more conspiratorial, I would probably surmise that this knowledge gap is being widened deliberately to exploit an ignorant mass, abusing democracy to consolidate and justify power under the notion of a mandate, but I won't go that far. *yet*


## Bridging the Gap

Everything we've seen in American politics the last decade has served as evidence towards this widening gap. While I'm scared of the chasm, what terrifies me are the possible necessary steps to bridge this gap.

The most obvious solution is to disenfranchise the uninformed. Simply prevent people who don't understand the socioeconomic and political landscape from voting. This can be done through a civics test of sorts. The scary part is we've already done this approach, and it was during the Jim Crow era. These tests directly disadvantaged low income individuals (often black) and immigrants, while also being ripe for bias in question selection and grading.

The other solution is the accelerationist approach, simply let the political environment become so unbearable that people have no choice but to become politically aware. Foster a revolutionary attitude in the populace, which has largely been absent in modern America because we've become all too comfortable.

We have unfortunately nearly reached a point where other solutions begin to seem impossible, as the conditions widening the gap are essentially self-perpetuating. We otherwise are trapped in a state of oscillatory purgatory, alternating constantly between fuax-populist politicians unable to fix democracy and actual fascism, all while the systems continue to degrade.

In ways, it would seem that the easiest way to form a more perfect democracy is to kill democracy, and rebuild it from the ashes.


## Conclusion

Within 24 hours, we'll likely see the results of the current midterm elections. In a way, this feels like our last chance at preserving the current system of democracy. Yet, in the back of my mind, I question if the current form of democracy should be preserved. At the very least, maybe abolishing the senate, and ridding ourselves of first past the post voting should probably be floated, but I can't forsee that changing without a drastic change in the political landscape.
