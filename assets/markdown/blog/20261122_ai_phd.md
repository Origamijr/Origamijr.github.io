We face quite a dilemma in academia. Just last week, an anthropic employee announced that AI had proven the general form of the Riemann hypothesis, one of the so called holy grail math problems that has stood unsolved for over a century. While this claim has yet to be verified by the community, it still sent shockwaves throughout the academic and non-academic world. In the background, there hung the existential question: if AI can solve frontier research problems better than humans, what is the role of humans in this world of AI research? Have we lost the romance of the field expert?

My up-to-date opinion on AI can be found in my [main AI opinion post](blog:LD_ai). I think myself as AI optimistic, but chronically worried about the negative impacts it's rapid mass adoption are having. As a current PhD student *hopefully* about to wrap up soon, I've glimpsed a bit into what research was like pre-AI adoption and post-AI adoption, and interacted with a variety of peers utilizing AI in many different ways.

Perhaps my disposition has led me to using AI more conservatively among my peers. For the longest time, even as AI got better at research and coding, I made the conscious attempt to limit my AI usage to finding gaps in literature review, and coding singular functions I couldn't help myself spending 10 minutes looking through documentation to figure out (classically this is mostly matplotlib and ffmpeg).

Things I have observed my peers doing with AI include generating figures for presentations, editing or even writing drafts, and coding entire experiments. A lot of these involve using AI to handle the more rote part of the scientific process, and I can see a clear justification for these uses.

Nevertheless, my hesitation around AI combined with seeing my peers use AI brazenly running laps around me has sort of put me in a tough mental space the past year or so, leading to a negative feedback loop stunting my own research progress. When I discussed this matter with my advisor, they expressed how we should try to be a bit more forward thinking, drawing analogies of the impact AI is having on research to the way Google search drew research away from libraries. This document has been long overdue as an outlet to organize my thoughts on the matter, and orient myself towards a more productive direction.

## The role of a PhD student

*Post writing I realize this is mostly from the perspective of an engineering/computing PhD student in academia. I cannot speak to the impact of AI in other fields/settings, though there are likely parallels that can be drawn regardless*

The question of the purpose of pursuing a PhD really harkens me back to the PhD application process, writing the statement of purpose. When I look back and read my applications, I'm somewhat surprised that I actually made it into a couple programs, only pointing at a vague notion of "continuing the journey of learning" and a "passion for knowledge". 

When I talk to many other current and prospective PhD students, the core reason for pursuing a PhD I often see is simply one of ego. We do a PhD simply because that's who we envision ourselves to be (at risk of projecting my thoughts onto others). *Though if you ask me why I'm doing a PhD I'll jokingly say that I applied to jobs and PhDs at the same time, and I got into a PhD program first.*

It's only really in hindsight, while in the middle of the program, that I think I have a better understanding what the role of the PhD student is, and what the goal should be. Fundamentally, a PhD program is a training program to give students the skills to become independent researchers. It's not to make field moving breakthroughs or to craft some magnum opus of a thesis. We train in the ability to ask interesting questions, weave those questions into narratives to convince people around us the questions are worth answering, effectively execute procedures to answer those questions to the extent we can, and wrap up the narrative saying that the question was worth tackling.

To a PhD student, the outcome of these questions is less important than the ability to iterate through this process which is half storytelling and half technical prowess. Without proper storytelling, the knowledge goes unsupported and unacknowledged. Without field appropriate skills, the stories are the empty promises of a charlatan.

To sum things up, from my experience thus far, I find the role of a PhD student is to **train** the following skills:

1. Have enough knowledge in a field to know what questions are worth asking, and to be a good enough academic peer to evaluate and discuss the works of others in the field.
2. Know the proper steps to attempt to answer questions within the field, along with the technical skill to do so yourself.
3. Be able to package these complex processes to varying types of audiences, and communicate those ideas well.

## The role of a PhD student with AI in the picture

In a way, AI has an ability to be injected and aid in the appearance that all three roles have been achieved. AI can do literature review and determine what gaps exist in research. AI can review papers and estimate their worth (to the detriment of basically all major conferences). AI can plan experiments, and if it heavily involves math and/or coding, pretty much carry out those experiments independently. AI can write papers and make presentations, both script, slides, and even videos (though I think this is where AI is weakest among the previously mentioned capabilities).

Altogether, AI is, at least at the surface level, already capable of what we are training PhD students to do. In this setting, is there a place for PhD students? I suppose that's sort of an existential question a lot of math and tech people are asking themselves at the moment.

### Role 1: Be knowledgeable

If AI is effectively knowledgeable, what is the purpose of deep human knowledge? For the sake of discussion let's just assume AI is in fact deeply knowledgeable. This is a tough question that I'll try to answer in the way I know best. As things stand, the primary gap between human and machine is in the sociological and biological needs humans have that machines don't. Also in the realm of these language models, these machines don't ideate within a physical reality as directly as we do.

Succinctly, this is just a humanist spin on the issue of alignment. A human and a machine with a set of similar knowledge will have different goals and ask different questions. So what if a less knowledgeable human prompts a knowledgeable machine with their human centric goals? I would contend that this is an ill-framed setting, as the ability to formulate goals in a way to prompt questions would necessitate a degree of knowledge in the first place.

So as it stands, PhD students still very much have the important role of being knowledgeable enough to know what questions are worth asking. 

### Role 2: Be capable

Given a question, AI is fairly capable in determining the steps needed to answer that question and executing upon them. So to what degree is a PhD student responsible in developing those skills to plan and execute those experiments independent of AI. I'd probably have a hard time arguing for the complete abolition of AI in the experiment execution process. 

However, so long as our questions are imperfectly conveyed to the machine (which is inevitable without having the machine literally read our minds), we will inevitably leave out context and methodological considerations in our prompts. Hence, it is the responsibility of the experimenter to know what procedure would answer their question, and ensure the procedure is being executed properly. This is ultimately impossible without a degree of personal skill of the medium through which the experiment is being conducted upon, for how can you verify procedural correctness without procedural knowledge.

I feel like this is the vector through which the impact of AI in research is most invisible. However, because it is invisible our systematic immune system is not properly responding to the potential harmful effects that will occur down the line. When we become over reliant on AI to perform certain skills in our stead, our ability to do those skills ourselves naturally degrade. So while I don't find too much fault in the use of AI itself to execute skills, we must be very careful not to lose those skills collectively.

### Role 3: Communicate well

In a way, I am both most sympathetic and least sympathetic on the use of AI on this front. One of the first things I realized stepping into academia is how privileged I am to be a native English speaker. I don't think I'm particularly good at writing, but I seem to understand a lot of the mechanics and subtleties a lot better than non-native English speakers. Although academia is worldwide, the dominant language is undoubtedly English, with the largest conferences and journals being in English. So to some extent, I understand the use of AI to smooth over imperfect English.

I also don't think AI is particularly ineffective at communicating. If anything, I think it's main fault is that most of AI writing is way too verbose (which in itself is a detriment to communication), but it can still convey ideas. So long as AI communication has a tell though (which it does), I think it's in the best interest of PhD students to try to learn to communicate without AI.

The argument is simply that communicating with AI signals a degree of incompetence on the only axis people can evaluate. The audience only knows what questions you ask and the approaches you took through the communication channels you employ. If it's apparent that very channel was not handled by a human, it begs the question of the capability of the human on all other aspects. After all, the goal is to position yourself as the expert they can rely on to be a tastemaker in the field.

So as long as a PhD program is a training program, and you find your communication skills insufficient (like I do), it really is the best interest of the student to use the training period time to actually train their communication skills. Using AI there is completely counterproductive.

## What I'll do

With most of my thoughts on the topic laid out, I'll try to state what I'll do with AI from this point on. I'll continue learning and reading existing papers to build my own knowledge. I'll continue writing my own papers and presentations without the use of AI (not even editing, I have personal proofreading, spellcheck, and peers for that). What I will change is I'll try to be less resistant on the technical front, allowing AI to take over certain rote tasks, but I'll try to do so carefully while maintaining my own skills. This will have to be a balance I'll have to learn, but learning is what I'm here for after all.
