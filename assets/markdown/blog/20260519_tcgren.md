 > "I am convinced, the way one plays chess always reflects the player's personality. If something defines his character, then it will also define his way of playing." -Vladmir Kramnik

Games can be said to provide a window into the mind of the player. There have been many TV shows where I see a character's chess tactics are said to reflect the personalities of the player. There's likely some extent this is true at a level of chess higher than where I'm at, but my experience has mostly been both players trying to select relatively locally optimally moves while not blundering.

Games can generally serve a purpose of self-expression, be it your cosmetics and class in an MMORPG, or your main in a fighter game. While many multiplayer games act as a medium for social interaction, board games take this interaction a step further with their "in person" elements. Many board games, like chess, contain a lot of built-in symmetry, so player expression is often defined by the play-by-play decisions. While some games like Root and Arkham Horror have a fair degree of player asymmetry, I don't think any board game genre epitomizes player expression the same way Trading Card Games do.

TCGs have been a pretty big part of my life, with me being part of the group in high school that plays yugioh at lunch everyday. While I fell off in college, I've still tried to keep up with the goings in the TCG world. In the past few years, I've started to notice some interesting trends that seem to indicate a change in the way TCGs are consumed and developed.

### A Very Brief History

*This is not really from direct research, just some rehashed history off the top of my head, so it's not too complete, but gets the gist*

Trading card games as we know them started with Richard Garfield creating Magic the Gathering in the early 90s. It's success led to a bunch of subsequent TCGs, including the Pokemon TCG (also originally from MTG's WOTC) and Yugioh (which we now call the big 3). This early-to-mid 2000s era contained a lot of copycats TCGs capitalizing on the trend. Except for the big 3, most games from this era ended up discontinued one way or another.

What I believe to be the second age of TCGs started in the late 2010s and the early 2020s. This second era is defined by three new factors. Firstly, we start to see players who grew up on TCGs turn into designers of TCGs. This resulted in several notable indie TCGs like Flesh and Blood, Sorcery, Grand Archive, and Elestrals. Second, social media amplified the hobby with the virality of card pack opening combined with the nostalgia of people who previously collected card games. Third, the overt financialization of the TCG market, particularly the Pokemon TCG. This third point in particular seemingly led to the current corporate TCG boom, including Lorcana, One Piece, Riftbound, and the recent record breaking kickstarter of the Cyberpunk TCG.

Quick note, this recap is mostly from the Western perspective. Notably, Japan's TCG market developed mostly in parallel with the Western market, but with a much different audience. My knowledge of the Japanese market is mostly surface level, since the English speaking internet does not cover that side as much.

### Current State of TCGs (opinion part)

I often hear that we are currently in a TCG bubble. In a way, I do agree with this sentiment. A sober look at the current market makes it seem that we have reached a sort of "max stupid", with so much of the market being propped by speculative value. While this mostly applies to the Pokemon TCG, a crash in the Pokemon market will inevitably lead to negative downstream effects on the rest of the TCG market.

Another worrying trend I see in the current era is a homogenization of TCG mechanics. I should be clear in saying that reusing tcg mechanics is actually mostly a good thing, insofar that it makes learning a new TCG from knowing another easier. Common mechanics include simple mechanics like drawing cards, card combat, and discard combats. We also see more complex common mechanisms such as "any card as resource", shield systems, and lane-based gameplay. The problem arises when gameplay-wise, a game becomes just "MTG but without lands", "Pokemon but better", or "yet another Duel Masters clone not again". I believe this homogenization is worsened by the increased financialization of card games, leading corporations to look for proven mechanics to vehicle for their new TCG to catch the current trend. To some extent, a reskin of an old game with QoL gameplay changes can initially be appealing, but history shows that these often get dwarfed by the big players in the market. All this is to say that the already small TCG playerbase as it is will likely not sustain the ongoing second wave of TCGs.

### Future of TCGs (speculation part)

If I'm so critical about the current state of TCGs, why do I think we're going to see another TCG renaissance? I'm going to need you to bear with me, but I think the answer is AI. This is for two very distinct reasons. 

The obvious reason, which I resent, is that AI makes it easier for people to make card games. This is in conjunction with the previously mentioned wave of previous TCG players who aim to transition into TCG developers. Lurking the HTCG space, it's clear that there is an influx of games heavily aided by AI. Most of this, of course, is slop, but I try not to fault the creators too much as making something is better than not. However, if anything, AI inducing a flood of TCGs should hopefully induce the notion that creating a TCG is a more possible venture than before. Combine this with the increase in youtube content of people explaining TCG design theory and the design process, TCG design has become much more accessible now than ever before.

The second reason links back to my preamble, in that the current AI wave seemingly has caused a movement of people seeking more human connection in a response to the more overtly detached world. In a way, the second wave may have indicated a slight "normiefication" of TCGs, normalizing TCGs as a valid hobby for all ages. Similarly, I think the current AI trends have made people more artist conscience, with TCGs being a prime way to showcase and distribute artwork.

Structurally and mechanically, however, I think the TCG market will likely make some very drastic changes. As far as TCGs go, I think we'll see yet another consolidation in the market that we saw in the 2000s. The market seems to have expanded far too fast for the current demand. The TCG model itself is sustained largely by local game stores, who can only support a limited number of games.

With more people attempting to create their own homemade and indie TCGs, I suspect most will eventually lean into a more ECG approach. With a larger pool of creators, we'll see more people have unique takes on TCGs, more frequently drinking from other wells. 

While TCGs are best played face-to-face, I also think that we'll see improved digital integration in the TCG space. With the pandemic, webcam play became rather commonplace, and will likely become an even more common venue for playing among geographically disjointed playerbases of smaller indie games. Ideally, digital augmentation of TCGs also lead to smoother gameplay presentation towards the promotion of TCGs as a spectator sport.

All-in-all, I'm very hopeful and enthusiastic for the near future of the TCG space.
